AI Healthcare Robot

Flagship Project

Problem Statement

In high-traffic and low-resource hospitals, patients often wait hours for a single consultation. This leads to delayed triage, inconsistent initial assessments, staff overload, and poor patient experience. Existing kiosks or apps do not combine continuous vitals capture with face-to-camera interaction and doctor handoff.

The core gap is doctor scarcity plus the lack of a scalable, physical first-contact system that can reliably collect physiological data and facial cues while keeping the human in the loop for diagnosis.

Goal: Build a physical AI healthcare robot that autonomously collects vitals, performs facial analysis, interacts conversationally, and connects patients to doctors remotely — with emphasis on sensor reliability, medical constraints, and ethics.

Complete Medical Check Demonstration

Full demonstration of the healthcare robot performing a complete medical check, guiding a patient through vitals measurement and interaction.

Solution Overview

A stationary, kiosk-style AI healthcare robot that acts as a first-contact medical assistant. From the user's perspective: the patient approaches, is detected by the system, and the camera adjusts to their height. They place hands on sensors and stand on the weight platform; the robot collects vitals, runs facial analysis, and engages in AI-driven conversation. Data is sent to a cloud backend where doctors can view dashboards and initiate video consultations.

This approach is practical because it prioritizes reliability and calibration over mobility — evolving from an initial humanoid concept to a fixed, deployable form factor.

Key Technical Challenges

  • Sensor calibration & reliability: HX711 load-cell drift and wiring sensitivity; MAX32664 stabilization delay and strict hand-placement requirements
  • Real-time coordination: Synchronizing vision, servo movement, sensor readings, and AI dialogue without blocking or lag
  • Camera height adaptation: Accurate face tracking across varying patient heights
  • Edge–cloud latency: Keeping interaction smooth while offloading AI and storage to the cloud
  • Medical signal noise: Rejecting inconsistent or physiologically unrealistic optical-sensor readings

Engineering Decisions & Trade-offs

  • Humanoid vs stationary: Humanoid was complex and unreliable for clinical use; stationary kiosk improved reliability and deployability.
  • MediaPipe vs custom CV: MediaPipe chosen for robust, real-time face tracking and faster iteration.
  • Edge vs cloud: Edge for real-time tracking and interaction; cloud for AI analysis, storage, and doctor access.
  • MAX30102 → MAX32664: Upgraded for better signal processing and integrated physiological calculations.
  • ESP32 + Arduino: Clear separation between communication and low-level hardware control.

AI Smart Glasses for the Blind

Assistive Wearable — Edge AI

Problem Statement

Visually impaired people rely on canes, guide dogs, or other people to navigate unfamiliar or crowded environments. These solutions detect obstacles at close range but give little sense of what is actually around a person, or what a specific object, sign, or scene in front of them looks like.

Goal: Build a self-contained, glasses-form-factor wearable that detects obstacles and hazards in real time and can describe a scene or read text aloud on request — fully on-device, with no cloud dependency for the safety-critical path.

Solution Overview

A Raspberry Pi Zero 2W paired with a Sony IMX500 AI camera runs real-time YOLO-based object detection directly on the camera sensor, classifying obstacles by distance, direction, and speed and issuing verbal alerts through a small I2S speaker. On request, the glasses capture a full scene and use Gemini for a richer spoken description or to read text — giving both immediate hazard awareness and on-demand understanding of the environment.

Power comes from a LiPo battery with a TP4056 charger and MT3608 boost converter, housed in a 3D-printed frame, with the full build kept under a $115 bill of materials.

Electric Mobility Systems

Entrepreneurship & Business

Problem Statement

Electric mobility must be affordable, efficient, and tailored to specific use cases — from mini-buses and tractors to scooters and small cars. Off-the-shelf EVs are often expensive and not optimized for local or niche applications (e.g. campus shuttle, farm tractor, last-mile delivery).

The gap is end-to-end hardware capability: power source selection, motor and controller sizing, accelerator and safety logic, and mechanical–electrical integration that is reliable and maintainable.

Goal: Design and build multiple electric vehicle prototypes with clear power flow, control flow, and component relationships — emphasizing hardware engineering and mechanical–electrical integration.

Click the image to watch the full "all vehicles in one video" demo.

Solution Overview

Four distinct electric systems were built and documented: (1) Electric mini-bus — 48 V Li-ion, dual BLDC motors with individual controllers, single accelerator, front/rear lamps; (2) Electric tractor — high-power GM motor (5 HP), Li-ion and optional lead-acid/solar, key switch and accelerator; (3) Electric scooters — two generations (24 V DC and Li-ion BLDC) with accelerator and lights; (4) Electric car — Li-ion pack, single controller, dual BLDC motors, accelerator, and lighting. From the user's perspective: one pedal and key (where applicable) control speed; power and control flow are explicit and consistent across platforms.

This approach prioritizes hardware clarity: power source → controller → motor(s), and battery → loads (lights), with control signals (accelerator, key) clearly separated.

System Architecture

Four panels: Mini-bus (48 V, 2× BLDC, 2× controllers, accelerator, lights) — Tractor (solar/battery, controller, GM motor, key, accelerator) — Scooters (DC vs BLDC versions) — Car (Li-ion, 1 controller, 2× BLDC, accelerator, lights).

Key Technical Challenges

  • Motor controllers: Sizing and tuning BLDC and DC controllers for torque, speed, and thermal limits
  • Power management: Battery selection, charging paths (e.g. solar on tractor), and load distribution (motors vs lights)
  • Mechanical–electrical integration: Mounting, wiring, and reliability under vibration and environmental conditions
  • Control logic: Single accelerator driving one or multiple controllers; key switch and safety interlocks
  • Affordability vs performance: Component choice (e.g. DC vs BLDC) and scalability across different vehicle classes

Engineering Decisions & Trade-offs

  • Dual motors + dual controllers (mini-bus): Symmetry and redundancy; one accelerator drives both for simplicity.
  • Solar on tractor: Supporting charge path; trade-off is added complexity vs off-grid capability.
  • DC vs BLDC (scooters): First version 24 V DC for cost; second version Li-ion BLDC for efficiency and performance.
  • Single controller, dual motors (car): Simpler control and wiring; both motors share one command stream.

  • Motor control: BLDC and DC; single and dual motor/controller topologies

Autonomous Mobile Manipulator Robot (coming soon)

Autonomous Robot with Arm

Problem Statement

Most robots specialize in either moving or manipulating, rarely both together with vision-guided autonomy. A robot that can find an object, travel to it, pick it up, and deliver it end-to-end needs locomotion, manipulation, and perception working as one coordinated system — not three separate demos.

Goal: Build an autonomous mobile manipulator that navigates a room on mecanum wheels, locates target materials with a pan-tilt camera, picks them up with a 6-DOF arm, and delivers them — coordinated entirely through ROS.

Solution Overview

Three independently-actuated subsystems run as ROS nodes and communicate over topics/services for modular, real-time coordination:

  • Locomotion: 4x independently-driven mecanum wheels. Mecanum drive kinematics convert a target direction/speed vector into per-wheel velocities, enabling forward, lateral, and rotational motion without turning in place.
  • Manipulation: 6-DOF robotic arm (6 servo motors). Inverse kinematics converts a target end-effector position into joint angles, executed as coordinated servo moves to reach, grip, and lift.
  • Perception: Webcam on a 2-DOF pan-tilt gimbal. Object detection/tracking keeps the target centered in frame, feeding live position data to navigation and manipulation.

ROS Control Architecture

Perception Node processes camera input and publishes object position data. Navigation Node subscribes to object/obstacle data and publishes velocity commands to the mecanum base. Manipulation Node computes arm joint angles via inverse kinematics once in range and executes the grip sequence. A Motor Control Layer translates all high-level velocity/joint commands into low-level PWM for wheel motors and servos.

  • ROS
  • Python/C++
  • Inverse Kinematics
  • Computer Vision / Object Detection
  • Servo Motor Control
  • Mecanum Drive Kinematics
  • Embedded Systems

Control Flow — Pick-and-Place Sequence

  • 1. Scan: Camera node scans the room and detects a target object
  • 2. Localize: Object position calculated relative to the robot and published to the navigation node
  • 3. Navigate: Mecanum base moves omnidirectionally toward the object, continuously adjusting from live camera feedback
  • 4. Align: Robot positions itself within the arm's reach envelope; pan-tilt camera keeps the object centered
  • 5. Grasp: Inverse kinematics solver computes joint angles; servos execute the reach-and-grip sequence
  • 6. Transport: Robot navigates to the destination point carrying the object
  • 7. Release: Arm releases the object at the target location
  • 8. Repeat: System returns to scan mode for the next object

Key Technical Challenges

  • Mecanum kinematics: Converting a target direction/speed vector into correct per-wheel velocities for smooth omnidirectional motion
  • Inverse kinematics: Solving 6-DOF joint angles reliably from a target end-effector position
  • Real-time tracking: Keeping the target centered via pan-tilt gimbal while the robot itself is moving
  • ROS node coordination: Real-time communication between perception, navigation, and manipulation nodes without race conditions
  • Closed-loop reliability: Sustaining the full scan → navigate → grasp → deliver loop without manual intervention

Engineering Decisions & Trade-offs

  • Three independent ROS nodes: Locomotion, manipulation, and perception developed and tested independently; trade-off is added complexity in topic/service coordination.
  • Mecanum over differential drive: Enables lateral strafing and precise alignment with the arm's reach envelope, at the cost of more complex kinematics.
  • Pan-tilt gimbal decoupled from base: Camera can track a target independently of robot heading, improving grasp alignment accuracy.
  • Motor Control Layer as a single translation point: Keeps high-level ROS nodes hardware-agnostic; only this layer talks PWM.

Results & Impact

Closed-loop autonomy

Object detection → navigation → grasping → delivery, without manual intervention

Omnidirectional navigation

Mecanum-wheel kinematics for forward, lateral, and rotational motion

6-DOF manipulation

Inverse kinematics driving a full reach-and-grip sequence

Why This Project Matters

  • Systems integration: Three independently-actuated subsystems coordinated into one autonomous robot
  • ROS architecture: Modular, real-time node-based control that scales to more subsystems
  • Applied robotics math: Mecanum drive kinematics and 6-DOF inverse kinematics implemented and working together
  • Full autonomy loop: Perception, decision, and action closed without human intervention

Multifunctional AI Robot

AI Projects

Problem Statement

Human–robot interaction in real environments needs versatile perception and intuitive control. Many robots support either vision or voice, but not both in a single pipeline — and few fuse face, body, and object tracking with natural-language queries and real-time motion control.

The gap: a single platform that sees (people, objects, color), hears (voice commands), reasons (e.g. "What do you see?"), and acts (Mecanum drive, servos, safety) with low latency and clear failure handling.

Goal: Build a multi-modal robot that combines vision, voice, and real-time control so it can follow, greet, answer questions, and operate safely indoors.

Solution Overview

A centralized robot platform with a smartphone as the main sensor and AI host: camera feeds YOLO and MediaPipe for object/person/face tracking; voice input drives conversation and "What do you see?"-style queries; an AI reasoning layer turns perception into behavior; and an ESP32 executes motor and servo commands over Wi-Fi. From the user's perspective: speak to the robot, point at objects, and it responds with movement and spoken answers.

This approach keeps perception and AI on a single compute node (smartphone/PC) while offloading only real-time actuation to the embedded controller — balancing latency and complexity.

Self-Driving Car Prototype

Extra Robotics Projects

Problem Statement

Autonomous vehicle navigation demands robust perception for pedestrians and obstacles in real time. Production systems often rely on LiDAR and radar, which are costly and complex to integrate for education and rapid prototyping.

The challenge is to show end-to-end autonomy — lane following, sign detection, person/obstacle avoidance — using a vision-first, logic-based approach that keeps cost low and the pipeline interpretable.

Goal: Build a cost-effective self-driving prototype with PC as brain, vision-only perception (YOLO + OpenCV), and ESP32 for motion control — no AI decision model, only computer vision and logic.

Solution Overview

A small-scale car with a smartphone camera streaming video to a PC over Wi-Fi. The PC runs Python, OpenCV, and YOLO: road/lane detection via HOG and Hough line transform, traffic sign and person detection via YOLO, and decision logic (move, stop, turn, slow) based on rules — no learned policy. The ESP32 receives commands wirelessly and drives four Mecanum motors; an ultrasonic sensor and buzzer handle close-range obstacle and emergency stop.

From the user's perspective: the car follows the lane, stops at STOP signs and for people, and avoids obstacles using vision and ultrasonic feedback.

Key Technical Challenges

  • Wi-Fi link: Video stream and command flow between PC and ESP32; handling packet loss and delay
  • Smartphone as sensor: Mounting, framing, and streaming stability for lane and object detection
  • Decision logic: Rule-based stop/turn/slow that stays safe under detection noise and missed frames
  • Ultrasonic + vision fusion: Emergency stop and obstacle feedback feeding back into the PC loop

Engineering Decisions & Trade-offs

  • PC as brain: Heavy compute on PC; ESP32 only for motion — keeps perception and logic in one place and avoids embedded ML constraints.
  • Vision-first, no learned policy: YOLO + OpenCV + rules; interpretable and debuggable at the cost of less "end-to-end" flexibility.
  • Mecanum wheels: Omnidirectional motion for tight turns and corrections; more complex than differential drive.
  • Ultrasonic as backup: Vision for semantics; ultrasonic for close-range safety and emergency stop.
